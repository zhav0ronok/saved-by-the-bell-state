
### 1.2.1 - What is a Gate? 
If you are at all familiar with [logic gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate), then **quantum logic gates** (which I will just refer to here as "gates", or "quantum gates") will be intuitive for you.

A quantum gate is a **unitary transformation** on one or more qubit. If you have taken linear algebra, you are likely familiar with unitary matrices, and unsurprisingly, quantum gates correspond to exactly those. 

> [!NOTE] Unitary Matrix
> A matrix $U$ such that its inverse equals its conjugate transpose[^2]:
> $$U U^{\dagger} = U^{\dagger} U =\mathbb{I}$$

For example, the quantum `NOT` gate corresponds to the matrix
$$
X\equiv \begin{bmatrix}
0&1\\1&0
\end{bmatrix}
$$
Which acts on a single qubit state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ by interchanging $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$:
$$
X|\psi\rangle= \alpha |1\rangle + \beta|0\rangle
$$
Equivalently, 
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1\\1&0
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha\\\beta
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}
\beta\\\alpha
\end{bmatrix}
$$
You can confirm for yourself that the matrix $X$ is unitary.

One interesting fact to keep in mind is that *any* unitary matrix can be a quantum gate, as unitarity is the only constraint!

### 1.2.2 - Classical Gates

This, I believe, is an important digression, as there are many parallels between classical and quantum logic gates, but they do not perfectly map onto one another. For example, while there is only one nontrivial one-bit classical gate (namely, the `NOT` gate), there are several nontrivial quantum ones. 

Another important distinction, which gets at the heart of why quantum circuits can't simulate classical ones directly, is that quantum logic gates are inherently reversible, whereas classical logic gates (ie. [[#`NAND` gate|NAND]]) are not reversible.
