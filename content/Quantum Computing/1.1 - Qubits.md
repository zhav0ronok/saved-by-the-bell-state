### 1.1.1 - What is a qubit?

In classical computing, a bit (**bi**nary digi**t**) is the most basic unit of information: a logical state that can be `0` or `1`. 

The term "state" may be familiar to you if you've already studied quantum mechanics, but if not, I encourage you to read up on it and return! 

In quantum computing, we will extend this notion so that we not only have our previous two possible states (which we will now call $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ instead of `0` and `1`), but also any linear combination of them:
$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle\hspace{1cm}\text{where }\alpha,\beta\in \mathbb{C}
$$
This linear combination of states is called a superposition, and the $|\;\rangle$ notation used above is Dirac notation, which is used to represent states in quantum mechanics. As you may expect, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ form an orthonormal basis for this vector space.

It is important to note that this qubit does not differ from the classical bit in the sense that it can be just anything between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. It exists only in this superposition until it is observed, and so the result of measuring a qubit will always collapse to either $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$. 

You may ask then, what the value is in representing a state in this way, or what the complex valued coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ (called amplitudes) even represent. The answer is that these coefficients represent probabilities. A measurement of the qubit above will yield $|0\rangle$ with a probability $|\alpha|^2$ or $|1\rangle$ with a probability $|\beta|^2$. These probabilities must sum to 1 as such:
$$
|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2 =1
$$
Because $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are both complex, they can be written as exponentials in polar form
$$
\alpha=r_{0}e^{i\gamma_{0}}\hspace{1cm}\beta=r_{1}e^{i\gamma_{1}}
$$
Taking the magnitude of these squared and adding them together simplifies nicely because $|e^{i\gamma}|^2=1$ in every case, so we end up with
$$
r_{0}^2 + r_{1}^2=1
$$
Writing out the state $|\psi\rangle$ with the coefficients in polar form,
$$
|\psi\rangle = r_{0}e^{i\gamma_{0}}|0\rangle+r_{1}e^{i\gamma_{1}}|1\rangle =e^{i\gamma_{0}}(r_{0}|0\rangle + e^{i(\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0})}r_{1}|1\rangle)
$$
The global phase $e^{i\gamma_{0}}$ has no impact on any physical measurements, therefore it can be discarded, so
$$
|\psi\rangle=r_{0}|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}r_{1}|1\rangle\hspace{.5cm}\text{where }\phi=\gamma_{1}-\gamma_{0}
$$
The remaining phase factor $e^{i\phi}$  is called the relative phase, and it cannot be discarded as it impacts physical measurements. 

Lastly, since $r_{0}^2+r_{1}^2=1$, we can use Euler's Identity ($\cos^2x+\sin^2x=1$) to simplify things by setting $r_{0}=\cos x$ and $r_{1}=\sin x$. Note that, instead of using $x=\theta$, we will use $x=\frac{\theta}{2}$. The reason for this is not quite trivial (involving rotations on the Bloch sphere, or even a group theoretical approach), and I will include a section on it later. We now have the elegant result:
$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle = \cos \frac{\theta}{2}|0\rangle + e^{i \phi}\sin \frac{\theta}{2}|1\rangle}
$$
#### Verifying this result
The standard half-angle trig formulas are:
$$
\cos \frac{\theta}{2}=\pm \sqrt{ \frac{1+\cos \theta}{2} }\hspace{1cm} \sin \frac{\theta}{2}=\pm \sqrt{ \frac{{1-\cos \theta}}{2} }
$$
Taking the magnitudes of these squared (since $\alpha=\cos \frac{\theta}{2}$ and $\beta=e^{i\phi}\sin \frac{\theta}{2}$) gives
$$
\left\|\cos \frac{\theta}{2}\right\|^2= \frac{1+\cos \theta}{2}
$$
$$
\left\|e^{i \phi}\sin \frac{\theta}{2}\right\|^2 = \cancel{ (e^{i\theta})^2 } \left( \frac{1-\cos \theta}{2} \right)=\frac{1-\cos \theta}{2}
$$
Summing them,
$$
|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=\frac{{1+\cos \theta}}{2}+\frac{{1-\cos \theta}}{2}=1
$$
$$
=\frac{{1\cancel{ +\cos } \theta+1\cancel{ -\cos \theta }}}{2}=\frac{2}{2}=1
$$
Evidently, this works out.
